The ferry to the island left twice a day, once in the early morning and once in the late afternoon, and if you missed the second one you had to find somewhere on the mainland to spend the night. Most visitors learned this the hard way. The harbor town had a single hotel, a few rooms above the bakery, and a campground that flooded whenever it rained, which on that part of the coast was often.

I first made the crossing in October, after the summer crowds had gone home. The boat was nearly empty. A man in a yellow raincoat sat near the stern with a crate of chickens, and two teenagers shared a pair of headphones on the bench across from me, each listening with one ear. The water was gray and choppy, and the island appeared slowly out of the mist, first as a darker shape against the clouds, then as a line of cliffs, and finally as a small cluster of white houses around a stone pier.

The island had about three hundred permanent residents. There was a school with eleven students, a shop that sold everything from bread to fishing line, a church that held a service once a month when the priest came over from the mainland, and a pub that served as the town hall, the post office, and the place where every important decision was made. The landlady, a tall woman named Edith, had lived there her whole life and seemed to know the history of every stone.

On my first evening she told me how the island had changed. When she was a child, she said, almost every family kept sheep, and in the spring the whole community gathered to shear them in a big barn near the lighthouse. The wool was spun on the island and sold on the mainland, and the money paid for the things the island could not make for itself: tools, medicine, and the occasional luxury like a radio or a new pair of shoes. Over the years the price of wool fell, the young people left to find work in the cities, and the sheep were sold off one flock at a time. Now only two farms remained, and the barn near the lighthouse had become a storage shed for the ferry company.

But the island had not simply declined. New people had arrived, too. A couple from the city had opened a small studio where they made pottery from the local clay, and their bowls were now sold in shops hundreds of miles away. A retired engineer had built a wind turbine on the hill behind the school, and on good days it produced more electricity than the whole island used. A marine biologist came every summer to count the seals on the northern beaches, and she had persuaded the school to let the children help her, so that every student on the island could now tell a grey seal from a harbor seal at a glance.

I stayed for a week. Each morning I walked a different path along the cliffs, and each evening I came back to the pub to eat whatever Edith had cooked and listen to the conversations around me. People talked about the weather, the ferry schedule, the price of fuel, and the health of their neighbors. They argued, sometimes loudly, about whether the island should build a new pier for larger boats, which would bring more tourists and more money but would also change the place in ways nobody could quite predict.

On the last night, I asked Edith what she thought. She was quiet for a while, wiping the same glass over and over. Then she said that the island had always been changing, even when it looked like it was standing still, and that the real question was not whether it would change but who would get to decide how. She said she trusted the people who lived there to make that choice, because they were the ones who would have to live with it.

The next morning the sea was calm, and the ferry left on time. I stood at the rail and watched the white houses grow smaller until they disappeared into the haze. I have been back several times since. The new pier was eventually built, a smaller version than the one first proposed, and the pottery studio now employs four people. The wind turbine still turns on the hill. And Edith, who must be well over eighty by now, still runs the pub, still knows the history of every stone, and still makes the best fish soup I have ever eaten.
